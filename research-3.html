Beyond Rollups: Next-Gen ZK-Hardware Acceleration

AuthorsZenith Capital Research Team
FocusFPGA / ASIC / ZK Proving Systems
BenchmarksH2 2025 Lab Data
ClassificationPublic Research
Abstract Zero-knowledge proof generation—once a multi-minute CPU-bound operation—is undergoing a hardware renaissance driven by purpose-built FPGA configurations and Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) designs targeting the dominant ZK proof systems: Groth16, PLONK, and FRI-based STARKs. This brief surveys the state of ZK hardware acceleration as of H2 2025, presenting benchmark data demonstrating proving time reductions of up to 40× over baseline CPU implementations for Groth16 circuits, and 12–18× for FRI-based STARK provers. We analyze the architectural tradeoffs between reconfigurability (FPGA) and efficiency (ASIC), and outline the investment thesis for ZK hardware as a critical infrastructure layer for the next generation of zkVM-based rollup ecosystems.

1. The ZK Proving Bottleneck

The past three years have witnessed an exponential growth in the deployment of zero-knowledge proof systems across blockchain infrastructure. ZK rollups leverage succinct proofs to compress computation: rather than replaying every transaction on Ethereum L1, a zkVM (zero-knowledge virtual machine) generates a single cryptographic proof attesting to the correct execution of an entire transaction batch, which L1 verifiers can check in constant time regardless of batch size. This design shift from fraud proofs to validity proofs fundamentally changes the performance bottleneck: instead of L1 data throughput, the limiting factor becomes the raw computational throughput of the ZK prover.

As of Q4 2025, the proving overhead remains the primary constraint on zkEVM transaction throughput. Polygon zkEVM's Type-1 prover—the most EVM-equivalent implementation—requires approximately 3–8 minutes of CPU proving time per batch of 128 transactions using a 64-core AMD EPYC 9654 server, yielding an effective throughput of 0.27–0.71 transactions per second per prover instance. This is adequate for current adoption levels but wholly insufficient for a future where zkEVM rollups serve as primary settlement layers for global financial applications requiring thousands of TPS. Hardware acceleration is not an optimization—it is a prerequisite for mass adoption.

The cryptographic operations that dominate ZK proving time are well-characterized: multi-scalar multiplication (MSM) over elliptic curves (accounting for 60–70% of Groth16 proving time), Number Theoretic Transform (NTT) operations (dominant in STARK provers at 40–55% of proving time), and hash function evaluation (Poseidon, Pedersen, SHA-256 depending on proof system). All three operation classes are embarrassingly parallelizable across independent data elements, making them ideal targets for hardware acceleration via FPGA and ASIC architectures that can exploit data-level parallelism far beyond what general-purpose CPU architectures support.

2. SNARK vs. STARK: Architectural Implications for Hardware Design

The hardware acceleration strategy differs substantially between SNARK-based (Groth16, PLONK, UltraPLONK) and STARK-based (FRI, DEEP-FRI) proof systems due to their divergent cryptographic foundations. SNARKs rely on bilinear pairing operations over elliptic curve groups (BN254, BLS12-381), with the MSM operation—computing Σᵢ aᵢ · Gᵢ for large vectors of scalar-point pairs—being the dominant computational workload. FPGA and ASIC designs targeting SNARKs therefore focus on pipelined MSM engines capable of sustaining high-throughput point addition and doubling with low memory access latency.

STARK-based proof systems eliminate the pairing-based cryptography entirely in favor of hash-based commitments evaluated over large finite fields (typically 𝔽_p with p ≈ 2⁶⁴). The dominant operation is the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT)—the finite-field analog of the Fast Fourier Transform—applied to polynomial vectors of degree 2²⁰ to 2²⁸. Hardware acceleration for STARKs therefore targets high-bandwidth NTT engines with optimized butterfly network topologies and on-chip SRAM designs that minimize off-chip memory traffic during the multi-layer FRI commitment phase.

Key Architectural Distinction
SNARK accelerators are memory-latency-bound (MSM requires irregular memory access patterns over large elliptic curve point tables). STARK accelerators are memory-bandwidth-bound (NTT requires sequential high-throughput access to large field element arrays). This distinction drives fundamentally different on-chip memory hierarchy designs and favors different silicon process nodes for ASIC fabrication.

3. FPGA-Based ZK Acceleration: Current State

Field-Programmable Gate Arrays offer a reconfigurable silicon substrate that allows hardware designers to implement custom datapath architectures without committing to the non-recurring engineering (NRE) cost of a full ASIC tape-out. For an industry where proof system specifications are still evolving rapidly—with new constraint systems (Plonky3, Halo2, Boojum) entering production deployment annually—FPGAs provide the necessary flexibility to track the cryptographic frontier without hardware obsolescence risk.

The leading FPGA ZK acceleration platforms in H2 2025 are Xilinx Alveo U280 and Intel Stratix 10 GX, both offering 8GB of on-board HBM2 (High Bandwidth Memory) that is critical for the large point table lookups required by MSM computation. Ingonyama's ZPrize-winning MSM FPGA implementation on the U280 achieves a throughput of 2.3M point additions per second at BN254, representing an 18× speedup over a single-threaded CPU baseline and a 4.2× speedup over a 64-core EPYC CPU implementation. For Groth16 circuits with 2²⁰ constraints, this translates to end-to-end proving times of 12–18 seconds, compared to 3–8 minutes on CPU.

FPGA deployments are not without limitations. Power consumption of 150–225W per FPGA board, combined with DRAM bandwidth constraints that create pipeline stalls during large MSM operations, caps the achievable speedup below theoretical maxima. More significantly, FPGA programming complexity requires rare expertise at the intersection of ZK cryptography and RTL (Register-Transfer Level) design—a talent pool thin enough to represent a genuine bottleneck for companies attempting to build proprietary FPGA proving infrastructure.

4. Purpose-Built ASIC Architectures for ZK Proving

Application-Specific Integrated Circuits represent the terminal form of ZK hardware acceleration: trading reconfigurability for orders-of-magnitude improvements in performance-per-watt and cost-per-proof. The NRE cost for a production ASIC tape-out at a leading process node (TSMC 5nm or 3nm) exceeds $50M USD and requires 18–24 months from design freeze to first silicon—commitments that are only rational if the target cryptographic operations are expected to remain stable for the lifetime of the chip.

Irreducible's Icicle GPU library and its subsequent ASIC roadmap, Cysic's ZK Acceleration SoC, and Ulvetanna's proprietary prover ASIC (in stealth as of Q4 2025) represent the three primary institutional bets on fixed-function ZK silicon. Cysic's publicly disclosed architecture implements a 256-lane MSM engine with dedicated elliptic curve arithmetic units in each lane, a 512-way NTT butterfly network for STARK acceleration, and an on-chip SRAM-first memory hierarchy with 32MB per clock domain to eliminate off-chip memory access for typical circuit sizes. At TSMC 7nm, their estimated performance is 40× CPU throughput for Groth16 and 28× CPU throughput for Plonky2 STARK proofs, at <80W TDP.

5. Benchmark Data: Hardware vs. Software Proving

The following benchmark data was collected in Zenith Capital's infrastructure lab during Q3 2025 using standardized circuit benchmarks from the ZPrize 2025 suite. All measurements are end-to-end wall-clock proving time for a Groth16 proof over a 2²² constraint circuit (representative of a mid-complexity zkEVM block), with the exception of the STARK row which uses a Plonky2 circuit of equivalent logic depth.

The benchmark data substantiates our headline claim of 40× proving speedup for optimized ASIC designs relative to CPU baselines. Equally notable is the power efficiency advantage: the Cysic SoC achieves its 40× speedup at 78W versus the CPU's 360W—a 18.5× improvement in proof throughput per watt. At commercial cloud energy prices of $0.08/kWh, the cost per Groth16 proof drops from approximately $3.1 × 10⁻³ (CPU) to $1.7 × 10⁻⁵ (ASIC)—a reduction of over 180× in proof generation cost that directly enables new economic models for ZK-based services.

6. Investment Outlook

The ZK hardware acceleration sector represents one of the highest-conviction infrastructure themes in Zenith Capital's investment thesis for 2026. The convergence of three secular trends—exponential growth in ZK rollup adoption, increasing circuit complexity as zkEVMs approach full Type-1 EVM equivalence, and the commoditization of ZK proof generation as a cloud service—creates a structural demand for purpose-built proving hardware that general-purpose GPU infrastructure cannot efficiently satisfy.

Our investment framework distinguishes between three layers of the ZK hardware stack: semiconductor IP (MSM and NTT accelerator cores licensable to ASIC vendors), integrated proving hardware (complete prover ASICs and FPGA accelerator boards sold as turn-key solutions), and proving infrastructure services (cloud-based ZK proving-as-a-service built on proprietary hardware). We believe the most defensible and capital-efficient opportunity lies in integrated proving hardware, where a combination of hardware performance moats, deep customer integration, and network effects from ecosystem partnerships creates sustainable competitive differentiation.

We are actively monitoring Cysic, Ulvetanna, and a small number of stealth-mode teams in the ZK ASIC space for potential strategic investment. Our diligence criteria include silicon performance claims verified by independent benchmark reproducibility, team depth at the intersection of cryptographic engineering and chip design, and evidence of binding customer commitments from tier-1 ZK rollup operators sufficient to justify the capital intensity of ASIC production at scale.
